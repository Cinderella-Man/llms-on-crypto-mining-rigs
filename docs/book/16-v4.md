# 16. v4: three models, three presets

> **In short**
> - v4 was released on 2026-11-16: the build of 2026-11-13, after three days of tests. On Flash-Next a coding agent's task takes 2.37 and 2.43
>   minutes against v3's 3.08 and 3.19 at equal work, and the model writes 27.61 and 26.67 tokens per second against
>   20.66 and 18.92.
> - Deep in a conversation the gap is larger. At 49,152 tokens v4 reads prompts at 180.7 and 180.8 tokens per second
>   against 70.3 and 70.6, and writes 24.26 and 24.14 against 10.66 and 10.98.
> - Flint and Ornith-1.5-122B write one token a step, so the new kernels for the check pass never run for them.
>   Their gain is in reading: Ornith's task takes 16.2 % less time, and Flint's first word comes 32.3 % sooner.
> - One switch gives Flint exactly the same numbers, moves Flash-Next a little, and moves Ornith about as much as its
>   file's quantization does. So it is on for two models and opt-in for the third.
> - Several ideas with good estimates lost on the rig and stayed out. Flash-Next did not reach the 30 tokens per
>   second I had hoped for.

Facts in this chapter are from 2026-11-16. It picks up where [chapter 15](15-toward-v4.md) stopped, on the evening
of 2026-11-11: one build with every change since v3 behind a switch, 21 of them never timed on a mining card, and a
line that had run the agent check once.

## 16.1 Ladders: timing what was written

**The idea.** [Chapter 15](15-toward-v4.md) planned to time the waiting switches as *ladders*: one build, the first
load runs the line as it is, each next load adds one switch. On 2026-11-12 three ladders ran.

**The work between two steps.** [Chapter 13](13-the-check-pass.md) found 7.5 ms on the host between the draft's last
pass and the next check. Eight switches attacked it, as six rungs:

| Rung | Estimate, ms a step | Measured, ms a step |
|---|---:|---:|
| Ask for a guessed token's table rows the moment it is guessed | up to 4.3 | −4.44 |
| Prepare the check pass while the draft's last pass still runs | 0.89 to 1.75 | −0.70 |
| Keep the draft's work plan between its passes | 0.95 | −1.03 |
| Limit a cache clean-up to the cells it can change | 0.67 | −0.33 |
| Yield to the server's request queue once, and only when a request waits | 0.23 to 0.37 | −0.11 |
| Three small changes in the draft's own passes | not priced | −0.60 |

Every rung was a gain. Together a step took 111.94 ms against 119.15, 6.05 % less, all of it host time, and the
reply stayed the same in every load.

**Reading prompts.** The second ladder replayed the 62 prompts of a recorded agent session
([chapter 14](14-reading-prompts.md)). Three switches read them in 247.11 and 240.02 s a pass against 305.38 and
295.68 in two loads, 18.95 % less, faster for every size of turn: each prompt batch recorded as one launch, the
draft's slimmer catch-up pass, and the draft catching up while the main model already reads the next call. A fourth
lost: a helper thread that fetches the lookup table's rows ahead made a pass 5.09 s slower.

A third ladder put four more settings on top: 243.95 and 233.12 s a pass against 247.52 and 240.27, 2.20 % less
together, each rung inside the spread. Three went in: the draft's own token table on its card, prompt batches
through the sparse attention, and prompt calls of 4,096 tokens. The fourth, a larger launch queue for the driver,
stayed out. It saved 0.80 s and left the host 171 MiB of free memory at its lowest, against 393.

**The check-pass kernels.** Six kernels of [chapter 13](13-the-check-pass.md) ran together in a served check pass:
85.970, 86.001, 85.958 and 85.991 ms against 87.760, 87.874, 87.757 and 87.840 without them. The stack gained
1.828 ms. Inside it, two of the six lost:

| Kernel | Change in a check pass, ms |
|---|---:|
| 3-bit products, each weight read once for all four tokens | −2.498 |
| The shared expert's three products as one launch | −0.494 |
| 6-bit products read once | −0.235 |
| The tile-by-tile version for wide rows | +0.307 |
| The small 32-bit products on a leaner routine | +1.341 |
| Everything else in the pass | −0.248 |

The sixth kernel covers passes of five to eight tokens and does not run in a check of four. The 32-bit routine
took two kinds of product. On the shared expert's one-row gate it saved 0.327 ms. On the router, a
matrix of 512 rows, it cost 1.668 ms against the general routine it replaced. A row limit now keeps the gate and
gives the router back: 0.420 ms off a pass. The tiles were narrowed to the products where they had gained, timed
again, lost 0.115 ms a pass, and stayed out.

**Why.** A stack that gains can hide a rung that loses. The stack of six would have shipped with 1.6 ms of loss
inside it. A ladder costs one load per switch and finds that the same day.

## 16.2 The long turns, closed

[Chapter 14](14-reading-prompts.md) ended with a loss: on the stacked line, turns with 1,024 or more new tokens
read slower than on v3. The cause was one switch that changed the shape of the graph between the middle of a prompt
and its end, so the engine rebuilt its memory plan on every request and the nine-card pipeline emptied each time.

The fix gives the graph one shape in every batch. On the rig it rebuilt nothing in eight requests and gave the same
replies. On the agent check, with the ladders' switches on as well, reading went from 73.2 tokens per second to
136.0 and 147.6 in two runs.

One habit came out of it. Every rig check of a switch now counts graph rebuilds per request and marks a switch that
adds them. This loss had been in the line for a day before I looked at the server's log.

On the evening of 2026-11-12 everything the ladders had passed ran the agent check, the same twelve bugs:

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Minutes per task, at equal work | 3.08 | 2.80 | 2.44 | 2.37 |
| Tasks passed | 11 of 12 | 12 of 12 | 11 of 12 | 12 of 12 |
| Writing, tok/s | 20.66 | 24.75 | 26.12 | 27.53 |
| Reading, tok/s | 65.5 | 73.2 | 136.0 | 147.6 |

The line of chapter 15 repeated its own result of the day before, 2.80 against 2.79 minutes. The one task the first
candidate run missed ran to the 12-minute cap in 35 turns; the second run passed it in 1.0 minute and 5 turns. The
agent draws its replies at random, so one task can take a short road or a long one.

## 16.4 Story: the better file was slower

**The idea.** [Chapter 15](15-toward-v4.md) left a new mix of Flash-Next's file that scored better on all three
texts at the same size. Its speed was an estimate: 5.91 ms a token more, priced on v3's one-token kernels.

**What happened.** On the rig a step with the new mix took 125.83 and 125.86 ms against 107.80 and 107.77 for the
mix it started from: 18.06 ms more, three times the estimate. An 8,128-token prompt read 17.43 % slower. On the agent
check the candidate line with it took 2.61 minutes a task against 2.44 and 2.37 with the makers' file.

**Why.** The estimate priced one token at a time, on v3's kernels. A step on v4 is a check of four tokens on new
kernels, and nothing had priced the mix there. Which products carry the 18 ms is not mapped yet. One suspect, a
format without a kernel that reads each weight once, was checked and cleared.

So v4 ships the makers' file again, as v3 did. The better mix stays a recipe until it costs less.

## 16.5 The last round: what lost

On the morning of 2026-11-13 I stopped starting new code. Five more changes were allowed in, then the freeze. Most
of what was timed that day had a good estimate behind it. Here is what the rig said:

| Idea | Its case | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| A draft length chosen while drafting, up to six guesses | est. 1.0 tok/s more, from recorded replies | 4.049 tokens a step against 3.29, but an est. 160.3 ms a step against 117.4: 25.26 tok/s against 28.03 and 26.16 |
| Drafts of four guesses | [chapter 13](13-the-check-pass.md): it pays if a checked token costs under 14.77 ms | 3.7305 and 3.7760 tokens a step against 3.2786 and 3.2461, at est. 152.44 and 151.11 ms against 118.74 and 121.71: 24.47 and 24.99 tok/s against 27.61 and 26.67 |
| One shared read for a check's four tokens in the sparse attention | a step grows 18.4 ms between the start and 8,192 tokens, and this should take part of it | a step 3.31 ms slower at 4,096 tokens and 16.98 ms slower at 8,192 |
| A different split of a prompt across the cards | gives 64.81 MiB of pinned host memory back | reading 1.39 s a pass slower |
| The shared expert's one launch, extended to five to eight tokens | fewer launches for longer drafts | est. 0.207 ms a pass lost at five tokens, 1.352 at eight |

Each of them is still in the build, off. A few small ones gained and went in. The draft's memory is planned once and
re-planned once after a change of cards, where it had re-planned 19 times. Half of a buffer on the last card, est.
160 MiB, is made only when a prompt needs it. The shared expert's last product runs on the multi-token kernel for
passes of two to four tokens, 0.174 ms off a check pass.

**Story: a second that came from the keep-alive.** A trace of a served reply showed about 1.25 s outside every
measured step. The keep-alive of [chapter 10](10-v3.md) was behind it. Under CUDA's lazy loading, the first launch
of a kernel on a card waited until that card's keep-alive loop timed out, about one second, at the first call of
every new graph shape. Ending the loop before such a launch took a 2,760-token prompt from 26,637.95 and
26,065.24 ms to 24,712.63 and 24,744.13. It is the same mechanism that made v3's `idle` mode wait 506 s, at a scale
small enough to hide since the release.

**The post-freeze line** ran the agent check twice: 2.35 and 2.44 minutes a task against the candidate's 2.44 and
2.37. The verdict was negligible at equal work, with a small gain in writing, 28.03 and 26.16 tokens per second.
The target for Flash-Next had been 30. The longer drafts were the road there, and both lost.

**Why.** Most of these estimates came from replays of recorded text or from a kernel timed alone. Each was right
about the part it measured: a longer draft does yield more tokens a step. What the estimate could not see is what
the longer check costs on nine cards in a served step. [Chapter 3](03-measuring.md) said it first: an estimate
tells you whether to try.

## 16.6 The freeze and two candidates

The cut followed the plan of [chapter 15](15-toward-v4.md).

1. **One more upstream merge.** llama.cpp had moved 114 commits since the base of [chapter 11](11-after-v3.md). They
   went in before the candidate, so that the release is tested on the code it ships with. Fourteen files
   conflicted. With no switch set, the merged build computes what the build before it computed, bit for bit, on all
   146 test models, once one upstream change to the CPU's attention arithmetic is set aside; with that change 143
   of them differ at rounding level.
2. **The first candidate** went to a mining card. Upstream's changed prompt kernel passed 3,969 recorded cases, and
   the fork's own kernels passed their sets with every planted fault caught.
3. **A second candidate.** One switch of the last round lost at five to eight tokens and gained at two to four. A
   tiny change limits it to the range where it gains, and that made the second candidate. Only the affected checks
   ran again.
4. **The four release files** were loaded on the second candidate and on the last build before the merge:
   Flash-Next with its draft, Flint in my 8-bit and 5-bit files, and Ornith. Every card held the same memory on both
   builds, and every reply at temperature 0 was the same bytes.

The second candidate is v4.0, released on 2026-11-16 with the binaries the tests ran on.

## 16.8 Flint and Ornith: stack, then split on a loss

**The setup.** Almost all of v4's code was written for Flash-Next. Flint and Ornith run without a draft model, so
they write one token a step, and the kernels for passes of two to eight tokens never run. With only the three
host-side switches that act on each one's path, both were level with v3 on the agent check: Ornith 2.57 and 2.45 minutes a task
against 2.52 and 2.54, Flint 1.85 and 1.82 against 1.81 and 1.82.

**The idea.** What was left for them were the prompt switches, proven on Flash-Next only. Testing each candidate
alone on two models, at depth and on the agent check, is many runs. So: turn all of them on at once, the *stack*,
and split it only if it loses.

**Ornith: the stack gained.** At depth it read prompts 15.1 % faster and wrote as fast. On the agent check a task
took 2.08 and 2.06 minutes against 2.52 and 2.54 on v3. The stack had one flaw, the story of the next section, so
the preset is the stack without one switch:

| Ornith-1.5-122B, coding agent | v3, two runs | v4's preset, two runs |
|---|---|---|
| Minutes per task, at equal work | 2.52 and 2.54 | 2.11 and 2.13 |
| Reading, tok/s | 61.1 and 58.4 | 103.0 and 105.9 |
| Writing, tok/s | 16.22 and 16.49 | 16.52 and 16.13 |
| First word of a task | 16.34 and 16.52 s | 8.43 and 8.45 s |
| The host's free memory at its lowest | 731 and 799 MiB | 1,225 and 1,228 MiB |

**Flint: the stack lost.** At depth it wrote 1.4 % slower and read 14.8 % slower than the host switches alone. So
it was split, one candidate a load. The cost was the keep-alive: alone it read the five prompt chunks 16.5 % slower,
20.1 % slower at 32,768 tokens, and wrote 3.0 % slower. This is the switch that gave Flash-Next 8 % and Ornith
16.3 against 14.9 tokens per second in v3. At depth both still need it: without it Ornith writes 3.4 % slower over
the six depths, and Flash-Next 9.9 % slower at 49,152 tokens.

Of the rest, staged hops lost on one prompt chunk and were left out too. Batches recorded as one launch, the
checkpoints on the cards and the taper were slower at no depth and went in. So did the switch of the last round
that keeps the memory plan, because Flint rebuilds its plan hundreds of times in an agent run. On the agent check:

| Flint, my 8-bit file, coding agent | v3, two runs | v4's preset, two runs |
|---|---|---|
| Minutes per task, at equal work | 1.81 and 1.82 | 1.72 and 1.81 |
| Reading, tok/s | 179.2 and 181.3 | 219.7 and 204.1 |
| Writing, tok/s | 48.55 and 48.02 | 49.32 and 46.99 |
| First word of a turn, median | 2.65 and 2.61 s | 1.74 and 1.82 s |
| Restoring a checkpoint | 292 ms, from host memory | 2 ms, from the cards |

Reading is 17.6 % faster and the first word comes 32.3 % sooner. Time per task and writing are level, as expected
for a model whose writing path did not change. On my 5-bit file, one run each the same night: 2.45 against 2.55
minutes a task, reading 195.6 against 169.1, the first word after 1.82 against 2.81 s, writing 52.01 against 51.77.

**Why.** A switch has no verdict of its own, only a verdict on a model. The keep-alive is a gain for two models and
costs the third its deep prompts; why it does is not explained yet. Stacking first is the cheap order: Ornith's
stack needed no split for speed, and Flint's split took two runs.

## 16.9 Story: "results unchanged"

**The puzzle.** On the deep lane Ornith's stack wrote another reply at temperature 0 than the plain line, at three
of six depths, the same in both loads. Every switch in the stack was meant to leave the numbers alone. One did not.

**A shortcut that failed.** The quick way to find it was a machine with newer cards, which costs no rig time.
With the same build, file and prompts, and the mining card's kernels forced on, three RTX 3090s wrote another reply
than the rig at five of six depths. Small differences in rounding turn a near tie early in a reply
([chapter 3](03-measuring.md)). A newer card cannot stand in for a P106 when the question is which text comes out.
The search went back to the rig.

**What it was.** The taper of [chapter 14](14-reading-prompts.md). It reads a short prompt call in batches of 64
tokens instead of one larger batch, 512 tokens on Ornith and Flash-Next and 128 on Flint, so the nine cards work on
it together. Alone it wrote the stack's replies at 8,192 and 16,384 tokens. Its description in the engine said
"results unchanged".

**How much it changes.** A reply at temperature 0 says only that something moved. So I scored the taper as I score
a quantized file ([chapter 3](03-measuring.md)): the KL divergence of its next-token probabilities against the
plain line, on 24 chunks of recorded agent sessions, with a repeat of the plain line as the floor.

| Model | The taper against the plain line | The plain line repeated | For scale: the file against a fuller version of the model | Same top token with the taper |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Ornith-1.5-122B | 0.047074 | 0.000062 | 0.051815 | 93.889 % |
| Flash-Next | 0.012663 | 0.000000 | 0.285104 | 96.863 % |
| Flint | 0.000000 | 0.000000 | 0.0656 | 100 % |

The scale column is the file against its makers' 8-bit file on agent text for Ornith and Flash-Next, and my 8-bit
file against the 16-bit model on Wikipedia text for Flint.

On Ornith the taper changes the numbers about as much as the file's own quantization does. Perplexity stayed
where it was, 4.0750 against 4.0730, and every task passed with it. So it is another rounding of the same
arithmetic, not a measured loss. But a preset must not move a model that far without saying so. On Ornith the taper
is opt-in: with it the first word of a turn comes after 3.05 and 3.27 s instead of 6.69 and 6.06.

On Flash-Next the change is 4.4 % of the file's own distance. On Flint the numbers are the same to the last digit.

**A rule that was too narrow.** Before Flash-Next's run I had fixed a rule: the taper stays if perplexity moves by
at most 0.1 %. It moved by −0.31 %, so by the rule it failed. Then I looked at the tool's own error on that
comparison: ±0.31 %. The band was a third as wide as the error of the measurement it judged, and the result was one
error away from zero. The taper stayed, and the rule is written down as the mistake.

**Why.** Sixty-four tokens at a time and 512 at a time add the same numbers in another order, as a check pass and a
plain pass do ([chapter 13](13-the-check-pass.md)). How far that travels differs by model: not at all on Flint, a
little on Flash-Next, far on Ornith. Why Ornith moves this far is open. So is whether the taper's rounding lies
closer to the full model or further from it; that needs Ornith's 8-bit reference.

## 16.10 What ships

One build, and a preset per model, as in v3 ([chapter 10](10-v3.md)):

| Model, file | What the preset adds to v3's | Left out, and why |
|---|---|---|
| Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, UkisAI's Swift-1.5 file as published | 33 switches: the sparse attention and its tables, the check-pass kernels, the work between two steps, the prompt path, the taper | longer drafts, the shared read, the prompt split: each lost on the rig |
| Ornith-1.5-122B, my 2- and 3-bit file | the three host switches and five prompt switches | the taper, opt-in: it moves the numbers |
| Flint, my 8-bit file | the three host switches and four prompt switches, the taper among them | the keep-alive: it costs deep prompts |

The answers stay the model's own, with the limits this chapter and [chapter 13](13-the-check-pass.md) describe.
With the same random seed, v4 writes v3's tokens exactly on Flint: 26 replies to recorded agent requests, 16,290
tokens in all, the same at every position. Flash-Next's text at temperature 0 differs from v3's, and Ornith's is the same at every depth
checked.

## 16.11 What v4 leaves open

- **The step where an agent works.** Between the start and 8,192 tokens a step still grows, and nothing has mapped
  that stretch with every change on. The map comes first; longer drafts wait for it.
- **30 tokens per second.** Flash-Next writes 27.61 and 26.67 on the agent check.
- **The better file.** It scores better and costs 18.06 ms a step. I do not know yet where.
- **Ornith.** Which way the taper moves it, and why this far. Its 2- and 3-bit weights still have no tuned kernel
  for reading prompts.
- **A hang on newer cards.** While searching for the taper, a load on the RTX 3090s stopped twice inside a prompt
  call, the host spinning in the driver. It has not happened on a P106. It reopens if it does.
- **The lessons.** The last chapter of this book is still not written.

## What to remember

- Time a stack as a ladder at least once. A stack of six kernels gained 1.8 ms and hid two that lost 1.6.
- Count what should be zero. A graph rebuilt on every request doubled the time of a long turn and showed in no result,
  only in a log.
- An estimate from one token does not price a step of four. The better file cost three times its estimate.
- Longer drafts yield more tokens a step and still lost, twice. The yield is half of the sum; the cost of the
  longer check is the other half.
- A switch has a verdict per model. The keep-alive is a gain on two models and a loss on the third.
- Stack the candidates first and split only on a loss.
- "Results unchanged" is a claim to measure, per model. The same switch moved one model by nothing and another as
  much as its quantization.
- A pass or fail band must be wider than the error of the measurement it judges.
- A newer GPU cannot stand in for the old one when the question is which text comes out.
